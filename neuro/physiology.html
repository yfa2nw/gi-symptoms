Embryology of the nervous system and its defects: induction, closure, division, and migration

The nervous system arises when the notochord induces the overlying ectoderm to form the neural plate, which folds into the neural tube by the end of week 4. The tube forms the brain and spinal cord, dividing into three primary and then five secondary vesicles, while neural crest cells that leave its dorsal edges form most of the peripheral nervous system and several other tissues. Failure of closure produces anencephaly and spina bifida, failure of forebrain division produces holoprosencephaly, and malformations of the hindbrain produce the Chiari and Dandy-Walker malformations.

Key points

  • The notochord induces the neural plate at the end of week 3; the notochord later becomes the nucleus pulposus.
  • The anterior neuropore closes on day 25 and the posterior neuropore on day 28; failure causes anencephaly and spina bifida respectively.
  • The alar plate forms sensory neurons and the basal plate motor neurons, separated by the sulcus limitans.
  • Neural crest forms peripheral neurons, Schwann cells, melanocytes, adrenal chromaffin cells, and enteric neurons.
  • Folic acid before conception prevents most neural tube defects; valproate raises the risk, and open defects raise AFP.
  • Chiari 2 malformation accompanies myelomeningocele, and Chiari 1 malformation is the commonest cause of syringomyelia.

Neurulation

At the end of the third week, the notochord induces the overlying ectoderm to thicken into the neural plate. Its edges rise as neural folds around a central neural groove, and the folds meet and fuse in the midline, beginning in the middle of the embryo and proceeding toward both ends. The resulting neural tube sinks beneath the surface ectoderm and forms the central nervous system. The anterior neuropore closes on day 25 and the posterior neuropore on day 28, often before pregnancy is recognized, which is why folic acid is recommended before conception. The notochord itself persists as the nucleus pulposus of the intervertebral discs.

Brain vesicles and plates

The cranial neural tube expands into three primary vesicles: the prosencephalon, mesencephalon, and rhombencephalon. The prosencephalon divides into the telencephalon, which forms the cerebral hemispheres and basal ganglia around the lateral ventricles, and the diencephalon, which forms the thalamus, hypothalamus, retina, and posterior pituitary around the third ventricle. The mesencephalon remains as the midbrain, around the cerebral aqueduct. The rhombencephalon divides into the metencephalon, forming the pons and cerebellum, and the myelencephalon, forming the medulla, both around the fourth ventricle.

Within the wall of the tube, the sulcus limitans separates the dorsal alar plate, which forms sensory neurons, from the ventral basal plate, which forms motor neurons, and it remains visible in the floor of the fourth ventricle. The spinal cord ends at about L3 at birth and rises to L1–L2 in adults because the vertebral column outgrows it, so lumbar puncture is performed below L2.

Germ layers and the neural crest

Neuroectoderm of the tube forms the neurons and macroglia of the central nervous system, namely astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and ependymal cells, together with the retina, optic nerve, posterior pituitary, and pineal gland. Neural crest cells leave the dorsal edges of the folds as they fuse and migrate widely, forming the neurons of the dorsal root, cranial, and autonomic ganglia, Schwann cells, melanocytes, adrenal chromaffin cells, enteric neurons, odontoblasts, parafollicular cells, and the aorticopulmonary septum. Microglia are of monocyte–macrophage origin and therefore mesodermal, as is the dura mater. Disorders of neural crest derivatives, the neurocristopathies, may affect several organ systems at once; in Hirschsprung disease, enteric neurons fail to migrate into the distal bowel.

When it fails

Failure of the anterior neuropore to close causes anencephaly, with absence of the forebrain and skull vault; it is incompatible with life, and maternal AFP is raised, often with polyhydramnios. Failure of the posterior neuropore causes spina bifida. In spina bifida occulta, the vertebral arches fail to fuse but the neural tissue is covered, symptoms are usually absent, AFP is normal, and a tuft of hair or dimple may overlie the lower spine. In a meningocele, a skin-covered sac of meninges and cerebrospinal fluid herniates through the defect, usually without deficit. In a myelomeningocele, the spinal cord herniates with the meninges, causing leg weakness, bladder and bowel dysfunction, and hydrocephalus, with raised AFP and an associated Chiari 2 malformation. Folate deficiency is the commonest environmental cause of neural tube defects, and folic acid before conception and food fortification prevent most cases; valproate raises the risk about tenfold and is avoided in those who could become pregnant.

Holoprosencephaly results when the forebrain fails to divide into two hemispheres, producing a single ventricle and midline facial defects that range from cleft lip to cyclopia; it is associated with SHH mutations, trisomy 13, and maternal diabetes. In the Chiari 1 malformation, the cerebellar tonsils lie at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum; it is often silent but may cause cough-provoked headache and scoliosis, and it is the commonest cause of syringomyelia. In the Chiari 2 malformation, the vermis, brainstem, and fourth ventricle descend, causing hydrocephalus and brainstem signs, almost always with a myelomeningocele. The Dandy-Walker malformation, the most common malformation of the posterior fossa, combines failure of the vermis to form with cystic enlargement of the fourth ventricle, and most patients develop hydrocephalus.

Syringomyelia is a fluid-filled cavity in the central spinal cord. It compresses the spinothalamic fibers crossing in the anterior white commissure, causing loss of pain and temperature sensation in a cape over the shoulders and arms while touch and vibration are spared. Besides the Chiari 1 malformation, causes include trauma, tumors, and arachnoiditis.

Neurons and action potentials: structure, channels, and conduction

Neurons receive input on dendrites, integrate it at the axon hillock, and conduct all-or-none action potentials to their terminals. The resting potential of about −70 mV reflects potassium leak and the Na/K-ATPase, and voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels drive depolarization and repolarization. Refractory periods limit firing rate and keep conduction one-way, and myelin allows fast saltatory conduction. Demyelination, sodium channel mutations, and sodium channel toxins disrupt this process, and sodium channel blockers exploit it. Here, we review neuron structure, membrane channels, the action potential, and their failures.

Key points

  • The resting potential is about −70 mV, close to the potassium equilibrium potential, because potassium leak channels dominate at rest.
  • At threshold, about −55 mV, voltage-gated sodium channels open, producing an all-or-none spike.
  • Sodium channel inactivation and potassium efflux repolarize the membrane, and inactivation causes the absolute refractory period.
  • Myelin from oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells allows saltatory conduction between nodes of Ranvier.
  • Lidocaine and phenytoin block voltage-gated sodium channels.

Structure

Dendrites receive afferent signals, the soma holds the nucleus, and the axon hillock initiates the action potential, which the axon carries to terminals that release neurotransmitter. Myelin, from oligodendrocytes in the CNS and Schwann cells in the periphery, insulates the axon, leaving nodes of Ranvier packed with voltage-gated sodium channels. Most neurons, including motor neurons, are multipolar; some are bipolar; and sensory neurons of the dorsal root ganglia are pseudounipolar.

Glial cells support neurons. Astrocytes buffer potassium, take up neurotransmitter, and with their foot processes help form the BBB; microglia, of monocyte origin, are the immune cells of the CNS; oligodendrocytes myelinate many CNS axons each, whereas each Schwann cell myelinates one PNS axon; and ependymal cells line the ventricles, with choroid epithelium regulating CSF. The BBB consists of capillary endothelial cells joined by tight junctions, supported by pericytes, astrocyte foot processes, and the basal lamina.

Channels and the action potential

Potassium leak channels make the resting membrane most permeable to potassium, so the resting potential of about −70 mV lies near the potassium equilibrium potential, and the Na/K-ATPase pumps 3 sodium out and 2 potassium in to maintain the gradients. When summed input reaches threshold, about −55 mV, voltage-gated sodium channels open and sodium rushes in, an all-or-none depolarization. Sodium channels then inactivate and voltage-gated potassium channels open, repolarizing the membrane, and slow potassium channel closure causes hyperpolarization. At the terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels trigger transmitter release, and ligand-gated channels generate postsynaptic potentials.

During the absolute refractory period, inactivated sodium channels prevent another spike; during the relative refractory period, a stronger stimulus is needed. These periods set the maximum firing rate and keep conduction one-way. In unmyelinated axons, depolarization spreads to adjacent membrane slowly, whereas in myelinated axons current jumps from node to node, saltatory conduction, more than 10 times faster.

When it fails

Multiple sclerosis destroys oligodendrocytes, MOGAD targets myelin, and NMOSD destroys astrocytes through antibodies against AQP4, with secondary demyelination, slowing or blocking conduction. Tetrodotoxin blocks sodium channels, causing numbness and weakness, and sodium channel mutations such as SCN1A raise excitability and cause epilepsy. After an axon is cut, Wallerian degeneration breaks down the axon and myelin distal to the cut, starting within hours, and regrowth, at about 1 mm/day, begins only after it is complete; within hours, chromatolysis swells the cell body and nucleus and breaks down Nissl bodies.

Pharmacology

Local anesthetics such as lidocaine block voltage-gated sodium channels in nerves, so pain signals fail to conduct. Antiseizure drugs such as phenytoin, carbamazepine, and lamotrigine reduce sodium influx in neurons, lowering excitability.

Relationships to other topics

. The action potential ends at the terminal, where calcium entry converts the electrical signal into chemical transmission.

Synapses and neurotransmitters: release, receptors, and clearance

At chemical synapses, an action potential opens voltage-gated calcium channels, and calcium-activated SNARE proteins fuse vesicles to release neurotransmitter. Transmitters act on fast ionotropic or slower metabotropic receptors, producing excitatory or inhibitory postsynaptic potentials that sum at the axon hillock, and are cleared by reuptake or enzymes. Glutamate is the main excitatory and GABA the main inhibitory transmitter, with acetylcholine, dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin modulating specific systems. Here, we review synaptic transmission, the major transmitters, their failures, and drugs that target them.

Key points

  • Calcium entry activates SNARE proteins, which fuse vesicles and release transmitter.
  • Ionotropic receptors are fast ion channels; metabotropic receptors act through G proteins.
  • Glutamate is the main excitatory transmitter, GABA the main inhibitory transmitter in the brain, and glycine inhibitory in the cord.
  • Acetylcholine is cleared by AChE; small-molecule transmitters are cleared by reuptake.
  • Benzodiazepines increase GABA-A channel opening, and cholinesterase inhibitors raise acetylcholine.

Synaptic transmission

An action potential depolarizes the terminal, opening voltage-gated calcium channels, and calcium activates SNARE proteins that fuse vesicles with the membrane. Transmitter binds ionotropic receptors, ion channels that act quickly, or metabotropic receptors, G protein-coupled receptors with slower, longer effects. Excitatory postsynaptic potentials depolarize the next neuron and inhibitory potentials hyperpolarize it, as chloride enters through GABA-A receptors, and inputs sum at the axon hillock. Transmitter is cleared by reuptake into the terminal or glia, for small molecules only, or by enzymes such as AChE. Electrical synapses pass current directly through gap junctions with almost no delay.

Neurotransmitters

Glutamate, acting on AMPA and NMDA receptors, is the main excitatory transmitter and underlies plasticity and memory. GABA, acting on GABA-A chloride channels and GABA-B G protein-coupled receptors, is the main inhibitory transmitter in the brain, and glycine is inhibitory mainly in the cord. Acetylcholine acts on nicotinic ion channels and muscarinic M1–M5 receptors at muscle, autonomic synapses, and the basal forebrain. Dopamine from the SNc and ventral tegmental area acts on D1 and D2 families, norepinephrine from the locus coeruleus drives arousal, and serotonin from the raphe nuclei modulates mood, sleep, and gut motility.

When it fails

Loss of cholinergic neurons contributes to memory decline in Alzheimer disease, and death of SNc dopamine neurons leaves too little dopamine in Parkinson disease. Low serotonin, with low plasma levels of its precursor tryptophan, is linked to depression. Glutamate excitotoxicity contributes to both, seizures arise when excitation outweighs GABA inhibition, and in hepatic encephalopathy, ammonia enhances GABA-A chloride flow. Increased presynaptic striatal dopamine function is the final common pathway to psychosis in schizophrenia. In anxiety, an overactive locus coeruleus releases more noradrenaline, which acts on the amygdala, cortical GABA is lower in panic disorder, and serotonin pathways are involved, which is why SSRIs are first line.

At the neuromuscular junction, myasthenia gravis targets postsynaptic nicotinic AChR or MuSK, causing fatigable weakness that worsens through the day, with ptosis and diplopia in 85% and thymoma in 10%; pyridostigmine, a cholinesterase inhibitor, is followed by immunosuppression. Basal ganglia pathways: direct, indirect, and the role of dopamine

The basal ganglia select and scale movement through two opposing striatal output pathways. In the direct pathway, D1 medium spiny neurons inhibit the GPi and SNr, releasing the thalamus to drive movement; in the indirect pathway, D2 neurons inhibit the GPe, freeing the STN to excite the GPi and restrain movement. Dopamine from the SNc excites D1 and inhibits D2 neurons, so both actions favor movement. Dopamine loss causes parkinsonism, whereas indirect-pathway loss and STN lesions cause chorea and ballismus. Here, we review the circuit, its failures, and drugs that act on it.

Key points

  • The GPi and SNr tonically inhibit the VA and VL thalamus.
  • The direct pathway (D1) reduces that inhibition; the indirect pathway (D2, GPe, STN) increases it.
  • Dopamine excites D1 and inhibits D2 neurons, favoring movement through both pathways.
  • Dopamine loss in Parkinson disease causes bradykinesia; indirect-pathway loss in Huntington disease and STN lesions cause hyperkinesia.

Circuit

The striatum, made of the caudate and putamen, contains GABA medium spiny neurons that receive cortical and dopamine input. The GPi and SNr form the output and tonically inhibit the VA and VL thalamus, which relays to the motor cortex. In the direct pathway, D1 neurons inhibit the GPi and SNr, releasing the thalamus and allowing movement. In the indirect pathway, D2 neurons inhibit the GPe, which then inhibits the STN less, and the STN, the loop's only glutamate nucleus, excites the GPi, restraining movement. Dopamine from the SNc excites D1 and inhibits D2 neurons, so both actions favor movement.

When it fails

In Parkinson disease, loss of SNc dopamine weakens the direct pathway and strengthens the indirect pathway, increasing GPi inhibition and causing bradykinesia, and dopamine-blocking drugs cause parkinsonism by blocking D2 receptors. In Huntington disease, indirect-pathway neurons die first, reducing GPi inhibition and causing chorea, and an STN lesion removes the GPi's excitation, releasing the thalamus and causing hemiballismus.

Pharmacology

Levodopa crosses the BBB and becomes dopamine, with carbidopa blocking its peripheral conversion, and dopamine agonists such as pramipexole stimulate receptors directly. MAO-B and COMT inhibitors slow dopamine and levodopa breakdown. VMAT2 inhibitors such as tetrabenazine block vesicular loading of dopamine, reducing chorea, and D2 antagonists such as haloperidol reduce hyperkinesia but can cause parkinsonism.

Nociceptors

Nociceptors are free nerve endings whose cell bodies sit in the DRG, or the trigeminal ganglion for the face. Lightly myelinated Aδ fibers, with small receptive fields, carry the fast, sharp, well-localized first pain and release glutamate, while unmyelinated C fibers, with large receptive fields, carry slow, poorly localized pain that conveys intensity and release neuropeptides such as substance P. They are activated by histamine, NGF, substance P, CGRP, potassium, serotonin, acetylcholine, ATP, lactic acid, low pH, and arachidonic acid, and by temperature extremes, high pressure, and tissue damage. Silent nociceptors respond only after inflammation sensitizes them and cause rest pain in arthritis. The brain has no nociceptors, so awake craniotomy is painless, and because nociception is detection and pain is experience, either can occur without the other.

Somatic pain from skin is sharp or burning and well localized, while pain from joints, tendons, and bone is aching and less localized. Visceral pain is carried by C fibers and poorly localized. Referred pain arises when visceral and somatic afferents converge on the same spinal segments, as when MI pain spreads to the neck, shoulder, and back. Neuropathic pain follows nerve damage, with spontaneous firing or altered conduction; allodynia is pain from a normally harmless stimulus, and hyperalgesia is an exaggerated response to a painful one.

Ascending pathways

Aδ fibers end in lamina I and C fibers in lamina II, the substantia gelatinosa. The lateral, neospinothalamic tract crosses in the anterior white commissure and relays in the VPL to S1, conveying location and intensity. The medial paleospinothalamic and archispinothalamic pathways project bilaterally to the reticular formation, PAG, and intralaminar thalamus, then to the cingulate, insula, and hypothalamus, carrying emotional and visceral responses. From the face, the trigeminal ganglion projects to the spinal trigeminal nucleus in the medulla, which relays through the VPM to S1.

Descending modulation

In the gate control theory of Melzack and Wall (1965), the substantia gelatinosa acts as a gate. Aβ and Aδ input excites inhibitory interneurons that damp dorsal horn projection neurons, which is why rubbing a wound dulls sharp pain, and cortical centers also control the gate, which depression holds open more often.

Ascending signals and opioids activate the PAG, which drives raphe neurons of the RVM with glutamate. These serotonergic neurons, the main endogenous modulator and the main target of supraspinal opioids, activate enkephalin interneurons in the dorsal horn, whose opioid receptors on nociceptive terminals open potassium channels and block calcium entry, reducing substance P release and suppressing C fiber input. A parallel route from the PAG to the locus coeruleus releases norepinephrine onto presynaptic α2 receptors. Opioids in the PAG inhibit local GABA interneurons, releasing the descending pathway, and the PAG can also facilitate pain, contributing to chronic pain.

μ receptors bind β-endorphin and endomorphins and mediate analgesia, euphoria, respiratory depression, constipation, and dependence; κ receptors bind dynorphins and mediate spinal analgesia, diuresis, and dysphoria; and δ receptors bind enkephalins and mediate analgesia and decreased gastric motility. All three are Gi-coupled, opening potassium channels and closing N-type calcium channels.

When it fails

Lateral medullary syndrome damages the spinal trigeminal nucleus and spinothalamic tract, causing crossed pain and temperature loss on the same side of the face and the opposite side of the body. Because spinothalamic fibers have already crossed, cord hemisection causes pain and temperature loss on the opposite side below the lesion, and spinothalamic damage in multiple sclerosis also causes this loss.

Chronic pain, including neuropathic pain and painful attacks, affects 80–100% of patients with NMOSD. CRPS follows minor injury or surgery with severe limb pain, allodynia, and changes in sweating and blood flow, as inflammation sensitizes nociceptive neurons and sympathetic drive sustains the pain. In central sensitization, peripheral and dorsal horn changes lower the threshold and amplify pain, so chronic pain becomes a disease in itself.

Pharmacology

Opioids such as morphine and fentanyl act on μ receptors in the PAG, RVM, dorsal horn, and peripheral afferents, opening potassium channels and closing N-type calcium channels, and in the PAG they inhibit GABA interneurons to release descending inhibition. Paracetamol inhibits central COX and strengthens descending serotonergic inhibition, giving analgesia and antipyresis with little anti-inflammatory effect. SNRIs and TCAs, such as duloxetine and amitriptyline, block serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake in the spinal cord, strengthening descending inhibition and relieving neuropathic pain, and duloxetine also treats fibromyalgia.

NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, aspirin, and celecoxib decrease prostaglandin synthesis by inhibiting COX-1 and COX-2, with aspirin binding irreversibly and celecoxib selecting COX-2. Gabapentin and pregabalin bind the α2δ-1 subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing excitatory transmitter release and relieving neuropathic pain, and ketamine blocks NMDA receptors noncompetitively, giving analgesia and dissociative anesthesia.

The four nerves

CN IX supplies its only muscle, the stylopharyngeus, sends parasympathetic fibers to the parotid through the otic ganglion, carries pressure and oxygen signals from the carotid sinus and body that slow the heart, and carries taste and touch from the back third of the tongue, forming the afferent limb of the gag reflex. CN X, from the nucleus ambiguus, supplies the palate, pharynx, and larynx and forms the efferent limb of the gag, and from the dorsal motor nucleus sends parasympathetic fibers to the heart, lungs, and gut to the splenic flexure; the left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks under the aortic arch, and the uvula deviates away from a vagal lesion. CN XI arises from C1–C5, rises through the foramen magnum, and leaves through the jugular foramen to supply the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius, and can be injured in posterior triangle node biopsy. CN XII arises in the floor of the fourth ventricle, leaves between the pyramid and olive through the hypoglossal canal, and supplies all tongue muscles except palatoglossus; its lesion deviates the tongue toward the lesion, with wasting and fasciculations.

When it fails

Lateral medullary syndrome damages the nucleus ambiguus, causing dysphagia, hoarseness, and a reduced gag, and medial medullary syndrome damages CN XII, deviating the tongue toward the lesion with opposite hemiparesis. Bilateral UMN lesions cause pseudobulbar palsy, with dysarthria and dysphagia but no wasting, and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury causes hoarseness.

The respiratory center: rhythm, chemical control, and failures of drive

Breathing is generated by brainstem neurons and tuned to metabolic demand. The dorsal respiratory group in the NTS is mainly inspiratory, the ventral respiratory group is mainly expiratory and contains the preBötzinger complex that generates the rhythm, and pontine centers limit or prolong inspiration. Central chemoreceptors on the ventral medulla sense CSF pH, which follows PaCO2, and supply most of the drive, while carotid and aortic bodies respond mainly to hypoxemia. Here, we review the brainstem groups, chemical and mechanical control, breathing in sleep, and the patterns and failures of drive.

Key points

  • PaCO2, sensed as CSF pH by central chemoreceptors, is the main drive to breathe.
  • Peripheral chemoreceptors respond mainly to low PaO2; the carotid bodies supply about 15% of drive.
  • The preBötzinger complex generates the rhythm and is where opioids depress breathing.
  • Cheyne-Stokes breathing follows heart failure and stroke, apneustic breathing pontine lesions, and Biot breathing medullary injury.
  • Opioids shift the CO2 response curve right; benzodiazepines and propofol flatten it.

Brainstem groups

The dorsal respiratory group lies in the NTS and is mainly inspiratory. The ventral respiratory group is mainly expiratory, and its rostral part, including the preBötzinger complex, generates the rhythm. In the pons, the pneumotaxic center limits inspiration and the apneustic center prolongs it. Inspiration follows contraction of the diaphragm and external intercostals, which lowers pleural and alveolar pressure, and expiration at rest is passive, from the elastic recoil of the lungs. The resulting rate is about 12 breaths a minute at rest.

Chemical control

Central chemoreceptors on the ventral surface of the medulla and in the retrotrapezoid nucleus sense CSF pH, which falls as CO2 crosses the blood-brain barrier, and supply most of the drive over minutes. Peripheral chemoreceptors respond within seconds, mainly to low PaO2 but also to CO2 and H+; the carotid bodies signal through CN IX and supply about 15% of drive, and the aortic bodies signal through CN X. PaCO2 is the main drive, and oxygen drives the center only in severe hypoxemia; when the receptors disagree, as in hypoxic hypocapnia, the hypoxic response wins.

Between a PaCO2 of 45 and 80 mmHg, ventilation rises 2–5 L/min for each mmHg. Hypoxemia below 60 mmHg and metabolic acidemia shift this curve left, opioids shift it right, and benzodiazepines, propofol, volatile anesthetics, age, and fitness flatten it. Hypoxia causes rapid, shallow breaths, which avoid the work of high volumes, and hypercapnia causes deep, slow breaths, which limit dead space.

Reflexes and sleep

Slowly adapting stretch receptors mediate the Hering-Breuer reflex, in which inflation inhibits inspiration; rapidly adapting irritant receptors cause cough and faster breathing; and juxtacapillary receptors respond to congestion and interstitial edema with faster breathing, all through CN X. In REM sleep, breathing becomes irregular, accessory muscles are paralyzed, upper airway dilators lose tone, and drive falls and responds less to PaO2 and PaCO2.

When it fails

Cheyne-Stokes breathing alternates crescendo-decrescendo ventilation with central apnea in cycles of 45–90 seconds and occurs in heart failure, where slow circulation delays the chemoreceptor signal, and in 20–40% of patients acutely after stroke. Biot, or ataxic, breathing has irregular clusters with unpredictable apnea from medullary injury and raised ICP; apneustic breathing has prolonged inspiratory pauses from pontine stroke or TBI; and Kussmaul breathing is deep, rapid, and continuous in metabolic acidosis such as DKA and renal failure. In the Cushing triad, raised ICP compresses the brainstem and causes irregular respirations with widened pulse pressure and bradycardia, as in intracerebral and subarachnoid hemorrhage.

In opioid overdose, μ receptors at the preBötzinger complex blunt the response to CO2 and hypoxia, causing pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression, and decreased consciousness, and benzodiazepines, inhaled anesthetics, and alcohol also depress drive. In congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, the center ignores PaCO2, so breathing fails in sleep. Central sleep apnea arises from high loop gain in heart failure or low drive from opioids, brainstem stroke, or cervical cord injury and is treated with CPAP or BPAP, and in obesity hypoventilation, retained bicarbonate blunts the central chemoreceptors. With intact drive, an asthma attack causes fast breathing and low CO2, and a normal CO2 signals tiring muscles; in COPD, oxygen raises CO2 through ventilation-perfusion mismatch and the Haldane effect, so saturations of 88–92% are targeted; and in neuromuscular disease, weak muscles cannot respond to an intact drive.

The cardiovascular center: the baroreflex and its failures

Arterial pressure is buffered beat to beat by the baroreflex. Stretch of the carotid sinus and aortic arch opens PIEZO channels in baroreceptor endings, whose signals travel in CN IX and CN X to the NTS. The NTS excites the CVLM, whose GABA neurons inhibit the RVLM, the source of sympathetic drive, and excites cardiac vagal neurons in the nucleus ambiguus, so rising pressure slows the heart and dilates vessels while falling pressure does the reverse. Here, we review the reflex, its other inputs and outputs, and the syndromes of too much reflex, too little reflex, and a medulla cut off or compressed.

Key points

  • Baroreceptor afferents from the carotid sinus run in CN IX and from the aortic arch in CN X, ending in the NTS.
  • The NTS inhibits sympathetic output through the CVLM and RVLM and drives vagal output through the nucleus ambiguus.
  • The baroreflex resets with sustained change, so it buffers only short-term fluctuations.
  • Vasovagal syncope and carotid sinus hypersensitivity are excessive reflexes; orthostatic hypotension reflects too little.
  • Autonomic dysreflexia follows spinal cord injury at or above T6, and the Cushing reflex follows raised ICP.

The baroreflex

Wall stretch opens PIEZO1 and PIEZO2 channels in baroreceptor endings, letting sodium and calcium in; myelinated A fibers track fast, pulsatile change and unmyelinated C fibers report sustained pressure. Carotid sinus afferents travel in the carotid sinus nerve, a branch of CN IX, with cell bodies in the petrosal ganglion, and aortic afferents in CN X, with cell bodies in the nodose ganglion. They release glutamate in the NTS, which also receives chemoreceptor and lung stretch afferents. The NTS excites the CVLM, whose GABA neurons inhibit the RVLM, which drives sympathetic preganglionic neurons and maintains baseline pressure, and it excites cardiac vagal neurons in the nucleus ambiguus and dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus. The ventral respiratory column sits within the RVLM.

When BP rises, baroreceptor firing increases, vagal output rises and sympathetic output falls, and HR and contractility fall while vessels dilate; when BP falls, the reverse occurs. With sustained change, the operating point shifts toward the new pressure, so the baroreflex buffers only short-term change.

Other inputs and outputs

Cardiopulmonary receptors in the atria, ventricles, and pulmonary vessels sense volume: stretch lowers renal sympathetic tone and releases ANP, causing natriuresis, while low volume raises sympathetic tone, RAAS activity, and vasopressin. The area postrema mediates angiotensin II, which raises BP without much change in HR, and the hypothalamus and insular cortex modulate long-term control. Sympathetic preganglionic neurons arise from the lateral gray column from T1 to L2 and release ACh onto nicotinic receptors, and postganglionic neurons release norepinephrine, with adrenaline from the adrenal medulla. Parasympathetic fibers leave in CN III, VII, IX, and X and from S2–S4, synapse near their targets, and release ACh onto muscarinic receptors.

When it fails

Vasovagal syncope, about 50% of syncope, follows standing, heat, pain, or stress, with sympathetic withdrawal causing vasodilation and vagal activity causing bradycardia. Carotid sinus hypersensitivity affects older adults, triggered by tight collars or head turning, and is cardioinhibitory, with asystole of 3 seconds or more, in 70%, vasodepressor, with a systolic fall of 50 mmHg or more, in 10%, and mixed in 20%; monitored carotid sinus massage confirms it, and pacing treats the cardioinhibitory type. Situational syncope follows coughing, micturition, defecation, and swallowing, and a trigger, lightheadedness, brief jerks, and rapid recovery favor syncope over a seizure.

Orthostatic hypotension is a sustained fall of 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing, as 300–800 mL pools in the legs and splanchnic veins. It occurs in Parkinson disease, early in multiple system atrophy and dementia with Lewy bodies, and in pure autonomic failure, diabetes, B12 deficiency, and with α- and β-blockers. In POTS, HR rises 30 a minute or more within 10 minutes of standing, or 40 at ages 12–19, without orthostatic hypotension, and afferent baroreflex failure after neck radiation or surgery causes swings between hypertension and hypotension.

In autonomic dysreflexia, after SCI at or above T6, a stimulus below the lesion, usually a full bladder (85%), drives splanchnic vasoconstriction, raising systolic pressure by 20 mmHg or more with headache, while vagal bradycardia and flushing occur only above the lesion. In the Cushing reflex, raised ICP lowers CPP, the difference between MAP and ICP, causing a sympathetic surge with widened pulse pressure, then bradycardia and irregular respirations, a late sign of herniation in intracerebral and subarachnoid hemorrhage.

Antithrombotic drugs and their reversal in cerebrovascular disease

Ischemic stroke is treated by dissolving the clot and prevented by antiplatelets, anticoagulants, and statins, whereas intracranial hemorrhage needs the same drugs stopped and reversed. Thrombolytics act on fibrinolysis, aspirin and clopidogrel on platelets, and heparins, warfarin, and the direct oral anticoagulants on coagulation, and each anticoagulant has a specific reversal. Here, we review these drugs by target, mechanism, use, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • Thrombolysis is given within 4.5 hours with BP below 185/110 and no bleeding contraindication, and aspirin waits 24 hours after it.
  • Clopidogrel is a prodrug activated by CYP2C19, so omeprazole and poor metabolizer genotypes reduce its effect.
  • Warfarin is used when a mechanical valve rules out a DOAC, has many CYP2C9 interactions, and causes skin necrosis without a heparin bridge.
  • 4F-PCC with IV vitamin K reverses warfarin, protamine reverses heparin, idarucizumab reverses dabigatran, and andexanet alfa or 4F-PCC reverses factor Xa inhibitors.
  • Statins treat large and small vessel stroke, and CYP3A4 inhibitors raise their levels and cause myopathy.

Dissolving and preventing clots

Thrombolytics, alteplase and tenecteplase, convert fibrin-bound plasminogen to plasmin, which breaks down fibrin so that the clot dissolves. They treat acute ischemic stroke within 4.5 hours of onset, provided BP is below 185/110. Alteplase has a half-life of minutes, so a bolus is followed by an infusion, whereas tenecteplase is more fibrin-specific and lasts longer, so a single bolus suffices. They are contraindicated by hemorrhage on CT; head trauma or intracranial or spinal surgery within 3 months; active bleeding; platelets under 100000/µL, an INR over 1.7, or an aPTT over 40 seconds; therapeutic LMWH within 24 hours or recent DOAC use; infective endocarditis; aortic dissection; and an intracranial neoplasm with high bleeding risk. Their adverse effects are symptomatic ICH, other bleeding, and angioedema, and because antiplatelets and anticoagulants add to the bleeding, aspirin waits 24 hours after thrombolysis.

Aspirin irreversibly acetylates platelet COX-1, blocking TXA2 synthesis, so aggregation is reduced for the 7–10-day life of the platelet; low doses spare endothelial prostacyclin. It is given within 24–48 hours of an ischemic stroke, continued for secondary prevention, and combined with clopidogrel for 21 days after a minor stroke or high-risk TIA. It is contraindicated in active bleeding, aspirin-exacerbated asthma, and children with viral illness, because of Reye syndrome. It causes GI ulcers and bleeding, ICH, tinnitus at high doses, and salicylate toxicity in overdose. Anticoagulants, other antiplatelets, and SSRIs add to the bleeding, and ibuprofen taken first can block aspirin's access to COX-1.

Clopidogrel irreversibly blocks ADP binding to platelet P2Y12 receptors, reducing platelet activation. It is combined with aspirin for 21 days after a minor stroke or high-risk TIA and used for secondary prevention. It is a prodrug activated by CYP2C19, so poor metabolizers respond less, and omeprazole and other CYP2C19 inhibitors reduce its activation. It is contraindicated in active bleeding and causes bleeding and, rarely, TTP.

Heparins activate antithrombin, which inhibits thrombin and factor Xa; LMWH acts mainly on factor Xa. Clots stop propagating, so they are used for DVT prophylaxis and for cerebral venous sinus thrombosis even when hemorrhage is present. UFH is given IV with a short half-life, monitored by aPTT, and fully reversed by protamine, whereas LMWH is given SC, renally cleared, and only partly reversed. They are contraindicated in active bleeding, past HIT, and severe thrombocytopenia, and LMWH in severe renal impairment. They cause bleeding, HIT, hyperkalemia, and osteoporosis with long use, and antiplatelets, NSAIDs, and other anticoagulants add to the bleeding.

Warfarin blocks vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1), so factors II, VII, IX, and X and proteins C and S are made in inactive forms, and anticoagulation develops over several days as existing factors clear. It prevents cardioembolic stroke when a mechanical valve rules out a DOAC. It is monitored by INR, with a target of 2–3, and metabolized by CYP2C9, so its interactions are many: metronidazole, TMP-SMX, fluconazole, and amiodarone raise the INR, rifampin, carbamazepine, and phenytoin lower it, and so does dietary vitamin K. It is teratogenic and contraindicated in pregnancy and active bleeding. It causes bleeding and, because protein C falls early, skin necrosis, so heparin bridges at the start.

Factor Xa inhibitors, apixaban and rivaroxaban, bind factor Xa directly, without antithrombin, and anticoagulate within hours. They are the first choice to prevent cardioembolic stroke in A-fib. They are given in fixed doses without routine monitoring, are metabolized by CYP3A4, carried by P-gp, and partly renally cleared, so strong CYP3A4 and P-gp inhibitors raise their levels, and rifampin, carbamazepine, and phenytoin lower them. They are contraindicated with mechanical heart valves, active bleeding, and severe renal impairment, and they cause bleeding, with less ICH than warfarin.

Dabigatran binds thrombin directly, without antithrombin, and anticoagulates within hours. It prevents cardioembolic stroke in A-fib. It is a prodrug, mostly renally cleared, and a P-gp substrate, so verapamil and amiodarone raise its levels and rifampin lowers them. It is contraindicated with mechanical heart valves and severe renal impairment and causes bleeding and dyspepsia.

Reversing anticoagulation

IV vitamin K restores hepatic synthesis of active factors II, VII, IX, and X, so the INR falls over hours. It treats warfarin-related ICH with 4F-PCC and the coagulopathy of liver disease with FFP. It acts too slowly to be used alone in ICH but keeps working after PCC wears off. It opposes warfarin, and IV doses rarely cause anaphylactoid reactions.

Clotting factor replacement uses 4F-PCC, which replaces factors II, VII, IX, and X, or FFP, which replaces all factors, and corrects the INR immediately. 4F-PCC treats warfarin-related ICH, to an INR below 1.4, and apixaban or rivaroxaban when andexanet alfa is unavailable, and FFP treats liver disease. PCC is a small volume and acts faster than FFP, which needs large volumes and thawing. PCC contains heparin, so recent thrombosis and HIT are contraindications, and both cause thrombosis, with volume overload and transfusion reactions from FFP.

Protamine is a positively charged protein that binds and neutralizes heparin within minutes, fully reversing UFH and partly reversing LMWH, and it treats heparin-related ICH. Too much protamine is itself an anticoagulant. A previous protamine reaction is a contraindication, and it causes hypotension, pulmonary hypertension, and anaphylaxis, especially with fish allergy or protamine-containing insulin.

Idarucizumab is an antibody fragment that binds dabigatran far more tightly than thrombin does, reversing it immediately, and it treats dabigatran-related ICH. It is given IV, acts only on dabigatran, and can cause thrombosis once anticoagulation is reversed.

Andexanet alfa acts as a decoy factor Xa that binds apixaban and rivaroxaban, rapidly reversing their effect in ICH, with 4F-PCC as the alternative. It is short acting, so a bolus is followed by an infusion. It causes thrombosis, including stroke and MI, and it also binds heparin-antithrombin, impairing later heparin.

Antifibrinolytics and cryoprecipitate keep or rebuild clots: tranexamic acid blocks plasminogen binding to fibrin, and cryoprecipitate replaces fibrinogen. Cryoprecipitate treats ICH after tPA, and an antifibrinolytic is used in SAH when aneurysm repair is delayed, for no more than 72 hours. TXA is renally cleared, so the dose is lowered in renal impairment, and it is contraindicated in active thrombosis. Both cause thrombosis, including cerebral ischemia, which PCC and hormonal contraceptives make more likely, and high doses of TXA can cause seizures.

Preventing recurrence

Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase, which slows cholesterol synthesis and raises hepatic LDL receptors, lowering LDL and stabilizing plaque. High-intensity statins such as atorvastatin treat large and small vessel ischemic stroke long term. Most, including atorvastatin and simvastatin, are metabolized by CYP3A4. They are contraindicated in pregnancy and active liver disease, and they cause myalgia and, rarely, myopathy and rhabdomyolysis with raised CK, raised transaminases, and new diabetes; myopathy is more likely with CYP3A4 inhibitors such as clarithromycin and azoles, gemfibrozil, and niacin.

PCC acts faster than FFP, so 4F-PCC with IV vitamin K is the choice for warfarin-related ICH.

Relationships to other conditions

Acute ischemic stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage. The same drugs act in opposite directions: thrombolytics, antiplatelets, and anticoagulants treat and prevent ischemic stroke, whereas ICH needs them stopped and reversed. BP targets differ too, below 185/110 for thrombolysis but a systolic of 140 after ICH.

Cardioembolic and large vessel stroke. Cardioembolic stroke is prevented with a DOAC, or warfarin with a mechanical valve, whereas large and small vessel stroke is prevented with an antiplatelet and a high-intensity statin, with dual antiplatelets for 21 days after a minor stroke.

Lowering blood pressure and intracranial pressure after brain hemorrhage

After intracerebral and subarachnoid hemorrhage, blood pressure is lowered to limit bleeding, delayed ischemia is prevented, and raised intracranial pressure is lowered. Dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers and labetalol control blood pressure, nimodipine prevents ischemia after SAH, and osmotic therapy lowers intracranial pressure. Here, we review these drugs by target, mechanism, use, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • After ICH, a systolic BP of 150–220 is lowered to 140, and in SAH systolic is kept below 160 until the aneurysm is secured.
  • Nimodipine is given to every patient with aneurysmal SAH for 21 days and improves outcome more than vasospasm on imaging.
  • Hypotension is nimodipine's main hazard, and it can itself cause ischemia.
  • Mannitol and hypertonic saline lower ICP within minutes by drawing water out of the brain.

Lowering blood pressure

Dihydropyridine CCBs, nicardipine and clevidipine, block L-type calcium channels in arterial smooth muscle, relaxing arterioles, so BP falls quickly and can be titrated, with little effect on the heart. They lower a systolic BP of 150–220 to 140 after ICH and keep systolic below 160 in SAH until the aneurysm is secured. Both are IV infusions, and clevidipine is broken down by blood esterases and lasts minutes. Severe aortic stenosis is a contraindication, and clevidipine is also avoided with soy or egg allergy and disordered lipid metabolism. They cause reflex tachycardia, headache, flushing, and hypotension, which other antihypertensives worsen.

Labetalol blocks α1 and β receptors, lowering resistance without reflex tachycardia, and is used in ICH and SAH and to lower BP before thrombolysis. It is given as IV boluses or an infusion and metabolized in the liver. It is contraindicated in asthma, heart block, bradycardia, and decompensated heart failure, and it causes bradycardia, orthostatic hypotension, bronchospasm, and hepatotoxicity, with additive AV block alongside verapamil and diltiazem.

Nimodipine blocks L-type calcium channels with a preference for cerebral arteries. Every patient with aneurysmal SAH receives it by mouth or tube for 21 days to prevent ischemia from vasospasm, and it reduces delayed ischemic deficits, improving outcome more than vasospasm on imaging. It is lipophilic, so it crosses the BBB, and it has high first-pass metabolism by CYP3A4, so CYP3A4 inhibitors raise its levels and inducers such as carbamazepine and phenytoin lower them. It is contraindicated with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors, and the contents of the oral capsule must never be given IV. Its main adverse effect is hypotension, which can itself cause ischemia.

Lowering ICP

Osmotic therapy with mannitol or hypertonic saline raises plasma osmolality, drawing water out of the brain across the BBB and lowering ICP within minutes. It treats raised ICP in ICH and SAH, and mannitol, at 1–2 g/kg IV, also treats acute angle closure. Mannitol is renally excreted and acts as an osmotic diuretic, whereas hypertonic saline expands volume. Mannitol is contraindicated in anuria, heart failure, and hypovolemia and causes hypovolemia, acute kidney injury, and electrolyte shifts, with additive kidney injury alongside other nephrotoxic drugs; hypertonic saline is contraindicated in hypernatremia and heart failure and causes hypernatremia, volume overload, and osmotic demyelination if sodium was chronically low.

In SAH, nimodipine prevents ischemia, whereas nicardipine and labetalol control BP.

Relationships to other conditions

Intracerebral hemorrhage and subarachnoid hemorrhage. Both lower BP and ICP with the same drugs, but their targets differ, a systolic of 140 after ICH and below 160 in SAH until the aneurysm is secured, and only SAH adds nimodipine to prevent delayed ischemia.

. Raised ICP drives the Cushing triad of hypertension, bradycardia, and irregular breathing, so osmotic therapy treats the cause of that hypertension rather than the BP itself.

Antiseizure drugs: mechanisms, choice by seizure type, and hazards

Antiseizure drugs either reduce excitation, by blocking sodium or T-type calcium channels, binding SV2A, or blocking glutamate receptors, or increase inhibition, by enhancing GABA-A receptors or blocking GABA breakdown. The choice depends on seizure type and on hazards such as teratogenicity, serious rashes, and CYP interactions. Here, we review the drugs named on these pages, with the treatments for status epilepticus, eclampsia, infantile spasms, and metabolic causes.

Key points

  • Status epilepticus starts with IV lorazepam or IM midazolam, followed by IV levetiracetam, valproate, or fosphenytoin.
  • Phenytoin follows zero-order kinetics at high doses, carbamazepine induces its own metabolism, and both induce CYP enzymes.
  • HLA-B*1502 is tested before carbamazepine in people of Han Chinese ancestry, and valproate doubles lamotrigine levels, raising the risk of SJS.
  • Ethosuximide is first line for absence seizures, vigabatrin causes permanent visual field loss, and topiramate causes kidney stones and acute angle closure.
  • Valproate is avoided in those who could become pregnant where possible, because it raises the risk of neural tube defects about 10 times.

Sodium channel blockers

Phenytoin and its prodrug fosphenytoin stabilize inactivated sodium channels, limiting repetitive firing. Fosphenytoin is given IV for status epilepticus after a benzodiazepine, and phenytoin treats focal seizures. Phenytoin follows zero-order kinetics at high doses, so small dose changes cause large changes in level; it is highly protein bound, valproate displaces it, and it induces CYP3A4, lowering levels of warfarin, oral contraceptives, and DOACs. Fosphenytoin is water soluble and can be given faster. Pregnancy is a contraindication, as are sinus bradycardia and heart block for IV use. Phenytoin causes gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, nystagmus and ataxia at high levels, SJS, megaloblastic anemia, osteomalacia, and fetal hydantoin syndrome, and IV phenytoin causes hypotension, arrhythmia, and purple glove syndrome.

Carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine block sodium channels in the inactivated state. They treat focal seizures and are first line for trigeminal neuralgia. Carbamazepine induces CYP3A4, including its own metabolism, so its levels fall over the first weeks, and it lowers levels of oral contraceptives, warfarin, and DOACs; oxcarbazepine induces less, and CYP3A4 inhibitors such as clarithromycin raise carbamazepine levels. HLA-B*1502 predicts SJS and is tested in people of Han Chinese ancestry, and marrow suppression and MAOI use are also contraindications. They cause agranulocytosis and aplastic anemia, hyponatremia from SIADH, more often with oxcarbazepine, SJS and TEN, diplopia and ataxia, hepatotoxicity, and teratogenicity.

Lamotrigine blocks sodium channels and reduces glutamate release. It treats focal and generalized seizures and Lennox-Gastaut syndrome. It is glucuronidated and started slowly to lower the risk of rash, because it causes SJS and TEN, especially with fast titration or with valproate, which doubles its levels; a previous serious rash is a contraindication. Estrogen contraceptives and enzyme inducers lower its levels, and it also causes dizziness, diplopia, and ataxia.

Lacosamide enhances slow inactivation of sodium channels and treats focal seizures. It is given by mouth or IV and mostly renally cleared. It prolongs the PR interval, which adds to other drugs that slow conduction, so second- or third-degree heart block is a contraindication, and it also causes dizziness and diplopia.

Rufinamide prolongs the inactive state of sodium channels, reducing drop attacks in Lennox-Gastaut syndrome. It is taken with food and has little CYP metabolism, though valproate raises its levels. It shortens the QT interval, so familial short QT syndrome is a contraindication, and it causes somnolence and hypersensitivity.

Calcium channels and vesicles

Ethosuximide blocks the T-type calcium currents in thalamic neurons that drive thalamocortical spike-wave rhythms, stopping absence seizures, for which it is first line. It has a long half-life and is metabolized by CYP3A4, so valproate raises and inducers lower its levels. It is avoided with succinimide hypersensitivity, and it causes GI upset, fatigue, headache, and, rarely, SJS and blood dyscrasias.

Levetiracetam binds SV2A on synaptic vesicles, reducing transmitter release. It treats focal and generalized seizures and juvenile myoclonic epilepsy and is given IV for status epilepticus after a benzodiazepine. It is renally cleared without CYP metabolism, so it has few interactions, and the dose is lowered in renal impairment. It causes irritability, aggression, depression, suicidality, and fatigue.

GABA

Benzodiazepines, including lorazepam, midazolam, diazepam, and clonazepam, increase how often GABA-A chloride channels open, hyperpolarizing neurons, which sedates and controls seizures. IV lorazepam or IM midazolam is first line for status epilepticus; benzodiazepines also treat alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal and acute vertigo, and clonazepam treats RBD and dystonia. Lorazepam has no active metabolites, so it suits liver disease, diazepam is long acting, and midazolam acts fast IM or intranasally. They are contraindicated in respiratory depression, severe sleep apnea, and acute angle-closure glaucoma, and they cause sedation, respiratory depression, falls and confusion in older adults, tolerance, dependence, and withdrawal seizures. Opioids and alcohol add to the respiratory depression, and flumazenil reverses them.

Barbiturates, phenobarbital and primidone, increase how long GABA-A chloride channels stay open. Phenobarbital treats refractory status epilepticus, and primidone, which is metabolized to phenobarbital, treats essential tremor. They are strong CYP inducers that lower levels of warfarin, oral contraceptives, and other antiseizure drugs, and they add to CNS depression. Acute intermittent porphyria and severe respiratory disease are contraindications, and they cause sedation, respiratory depression, and cognitive slowing.

Vigabatrin irreversibly blocks GABA transaminase, raising brain GABA, and it treats West syndrome, with ACTH as the alternative. It is renally cleared, and its effect outlasts the drug because the enzyme must be remade; it lowers phenytoin levels. It causes permanent concentric visual field loss, so vision is monitored and pre-existing field loss is a contraindication, as well as sedation.

Several mechanisms

Valproate blocks sodium channels and, weakly, T-type calcium channels while raising GABA, giving broad seizure control. It treats generalized seizures, juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, and Lennox-Gastaut syndrome and is given IV for status epilepticus after a benzodiazepine. It is highly protein bound and inhibits hepatic enzymes, raising lamotrigine and phenobarbital levels, and carbapenems sharply lower its own levels. It is avoided in pregnancy and those who could become pregnant where possible, in liver disease, and in urea cycle and mitochondrial disorders such as POLG mutations. It raises the risk of neural tube defects about 10 times and causes hepatotoxicity, especially under age 2, pancreatitis, tremor, weight gain, hair loss, thrombocytopenia, and hyperammonemia.

Topiramate blocks sodium channels and glutamate receptors while enhancing GABA-A receptors, and it also inhibits carbonic anhydrase. It treats focal and generalized seizures, is mostly renally cleared, and at higher doses lowers estrogen contraceptive levels. Metabolic acidosis is a contraindication, and it causes cognitive slowing and word-finding difficulty, paresthesia, weight loss, kidney stones, metabolic acidosis, and acute angle-closure glaucoma.

Special situations

ACTH raises cortisol and also acts directly on melanocortin receptors in the brain, stopping infantile spasms and improving the EEG, and it is first line for West syndrome with vigabatrin. It is injected over weeks. Uncontrolled hypertension and active infection are contraindications, and it causes hypertension, hyperglycemia, irritability, infection, and cushingoid features.

Magnesium sulfate antagonizes calcium and blocks NMDA receptors, depressing neuromuscular and CNS excitability, and it prevents and treats eclamptic seizures, with delivery. It is given IV and renally cleared, so it accumulates in renal impairment, and it potentiates neuromuscular blockers. Myasthenia gravis and heart block are contraindications. Toxicity first removes reflexes, then causes respiratory depression and cardiac arrest, and calcium gluconate reverses it.

Thiamine and glucose restore the cofactor of glucose metabolism and the glucose itself. Glucose is given for seizures with low glucose, with thiamine when alcohol use is likely, and thiamine treats seizures from decreased vitamin B1 and prevents Wernicke encephalopathy. Thiamine is given IV because absorption is poor with alcohol use, and IV doses rarely cause anaphylaxis.

Valproate is avoided in those who could become pregnant where possible, because it is teratogenic.

Review article

Immunotherapy for multiple sclerosis, NMOSD, and MOGAD

Acute attacks of demyelinating disease are treated with high-dose corticosteroids, and relapses are prevented by drugs ranging from moderate-efficacy injectables and oral agents to high-efficacy antibodies and induction therapy. NMOSD responds to B-cell depletion, IL-6 receptor blockade, complement inhibition, and broad immunosuppression, and some MS drugs worsen it. Here, we review these drugs by target, mechanism, use, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • MS relapses are treated with IV methylprednisolone for 3–7 days, and NMOSD and MOGAD attacks with 1 g daily.
  • Natalizumab risks PML from JC virus, so JC virus status is checked first, and S1P receptor modulators cause first-dose bradycardia and AV block.
  • B-cell depleters need HBV screening, eculizumab needs meningococcal vaccination, and alemtuzumab causes autoimmune thyroid disease, ITP, and anti-GBM disease.
  • Interferon beta, natalizumab, and other MS drugs can worsen NMOSD, which is treated with rituximab, IL-6 receptor blockers, eculizumab, or azathioprine.

Acute attacks

Corticosteroids, including methylprednisolone, prednisone, and prednisolone, enter the nucleus through the glucocorticoid receptor, decreasing inflammatory gene transcription and BBB leakiness, so relapses recover faster. IV methylprednisolone is given for 3–7 days in MS relapse and 1 g daily in NMOSD and MOGAD attacks, oral steroids bridge in MOGAD, prednisone 1 mg/kg treats sudden SNHL, and steroids treat autoimmune encephalitis. High-dose IV pulses act over days, and prednisone is activated in the liver to prednisolone. Active untreated infection is a contraindication. Pulses cause hyperglycemia, insomnia, mood change, and hypertension, and long use causes osteoporosis, infection, cataracts, adrenal suppression, and avascular necrosis. NSAIDs add to the GI bleeding, and CYP3A4 inducers lower their levels.

IVIG, pooled IgG, saturates FcRn, speeding autoantibody clearance, while blocking Fc receptors and complement, so antibody-mediated injury lessens. It prevents MOGAD relapses at 1–2 g/kg every 4 weeks and treats autoimmune encephalitis. It is infused IV and its effect lasts weeks, and it blunts responses to live vaccines. IgA deficiency with anti-IgA antibodies is a contraindication, and it causes headache and aseptic meningitis, thrombosis, renal failure with sucrose-containing products, hemolysis, and anaphylaxis.

Moderate efficacy

Interferon beta acts on type I interferon receptors, shifting cytokines toward anti-inflammatory signals and reducing T-cell migration across the BBB, so relapses are fewer. It treats relapsing MS by SC or IM injection but is avoided in NMOSD. Severe depression and decompensated liver disease are contraindications, and it causes flu-like symptoms, injection site reactions, depression, raised transaminases, and cytopenias, with additive marrow suppression.

Glatiramer acetate resembles myelin basic protein and acts as a decoy, shifting T cells toward regulatory types and reducing relapses. It treats relapsing MS and is safe in pregnancy. It is injected SC daily or three times weekly and has few interactions. Hypersensitivity to it or mannitol is a contraindication, and it causes injection site reactions, lipoatrophy, and brief flushing, chest tightness, and palpitations after injection.

Dimethyl fumarate activates the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway, reducing oxidative stress and inflammation, and treats relapsing MS, taken by mouth twice daily. Lymphopenia is a contraindication, and it causes flushing, which aspirin before doses reduces, GI upset, lymphopenia, and, rarely, PML.

Teriflunomide inhibits DHODH in pyrimidine synthesis, slowing proliferation of activated lymphocytes, and treats relapsing MS. It has a very long half-life, and cholestyramine speeds its elimination; it affects the INR on warfarin variably. It is teratogenic, so pregnancy is a contraindication, as is severe liver disease, and it causes hepatotoxicity, hair thinning, and diarrhea.

High efficacy

S1P receptor modulators, fingolimod, siponimod, ozanimod, and ponesimod, bind S1PR1 on lymphocytes, which is internalized, trapping lymphocytes in lymph nodes so fewer reach the CNS. They treat relapsing MS but are avoided in NMOSD. They are oral; fingolimod is a prodrug activated by phosphorylation, and the newer drugs are more selective. MI, unstable angina, stroke, TIA, or decompensated heart failure within 6 months and heart block without a pacemaker are contraindications. They cause first-dose bradycardia and AV block, which beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers worsen, macular edema, infections including VZV, raised transaminases, and rebound disease on stopping.

Natalizumab blocks α4 integrin on lymphocytes, stopping them from crossing the BBB, and is highly effective in preventing relapses of highly active relapsing MS, given IV every 4 weeks; it is avoided in NMOSD. It can cause PML from JC virus, so JC virus status is checked first, past PML and immunosuppression are contraindications, and other immunosuppressants raise the risk. It also causes infusion reactions and hepatotoxicity.

B-cell depleters, ocrelizumab and rituximab against CD20 and inebilizumab against CD19, deplete B cells through antibody- and complement-mediated killing, reducing relapses and slowing disability. Ocrelizumab treats relapsing and primary progressive MS; rituximab every 6 months is first line in NMOSD, as is inebilizumab, which also removes plasmablasts carrying CD19; and rituximab treats MOGAD, though less effectively. They are given IV every 6 months. Active HBV, screened for first, and active infection are contraindications. They cause infusion reactions, infections, HBV reactivation, and low immunoglobulins, which are checked before treatment and yearly, and vaccines are given beforehand because responses are poor.

Induction

Alemtuzumab depletes T and B cells through CD52, and they repopulate over months, giving a long-lasting immune reset in highly active MS. It is given as two IV courses a year apart, and HIV infection is a contraindication. Months to years later it causes autoimmune thyroid disease, ITP, and anti-GBM disease, and it also causes infusion reactions and stroke.

Mitoxantrone intercalates into DNA and inhibits topoisomerase II, killing dividing immune cells, and is used for short-term induction in aggressive MS. It is given IV with a capped lifetime dose. Reduced left ventricular function is a contraindication, and it causes cardiotoxicity and therapy-related leukemia.

Cladribine, a purine analog, accumulates in lymphocytes and kills them, selectively depleting lymphocytes in highly active MS. It is taken as two short courses of tablets a year apart. Active malignancy, HIV, TB, and pregnancy are contraindications, and it causes lymphopenia, infections including herpes zoster, and possibly malignancy.

NMOSD and broad immunosuppression

IL-6 receptor blockers, satralizumab and tocilizumab, block IL-6 signaling, lowering plasmablast survival and AQP4-IgG production, so NMOSD relapses are fewer in patients with AQP4-IgG. Satralizumab is given SC monthly and tocilizumab IV or SC. Because IL-6 suppresses CYP enzymes, blocking it can lower levels of CYP3A4 substrates. Active infection including TB is a contraindication, and they cause infections, raised transaminases and cholesterol, and neutropenia.

Eculizumab blocks cleavage of complement C5, preventing the membrane attack complex and so antibody-mediated astrocyte injury in NMOSD with AQP4-IgG. It is given IV every 2 weeks after loading. It causes meningococcal infection, which can be fatal, so vaccination comes first, and lack of vaccination or active meningococcal infection is a contraindication.

Broad immunosuppressants block lymphocyte proliferation in different ways: azathioprine blocks purine synthesis, MMF does so through IMPDH, methotrexate blocks folate metabolism, and tacrolimus blocks calcineurin. Azathioprine with prednisone is first line in NMOSD, and these drugs prevent MOGAD relapses, reducing relapses after months. Their onset is slow, so steroids bridge; TPMT is tested before azathioprine, and allopurinol raises its levels; tacrolimus levels are monitored, and CYP3A4 inhibitors raise them. MMF and methotrexate are contraindicated in pregnancy, and azathioprine with low TPMT activity. All cause marrow suppression, infection, and malignancy; azathioprine also causes hepatotoxicity and pancreatitis, and tacrolimus nephrotoxicity, tremor, and diabetes.

Interferon beta, natalizumab, and other MS drugs can worsen NMOSD, so the diagnosis is settled before choosing one.

Relationships to other conditions

Multiple sclerosis and NMOSD. Both treat attacks with IV methylprednisolone and plasma exchange, but their preventive drugs differ. MS uses interferons, glatiramer, oral drugs, S1P receptor modulators, natalizumab, and anti-CD20 drugs, by escalation or early high efficacy, whereas NMOSD uses B-cell depletion, IL-6 receptor blockers, eculizumab, or azathioprine, and interferon beta and natalizumab can worsen it.

MOGAD. Relapse prevention usually starts after a second attack, with azathioprine or MMF under a prednisolone bridge, IVIG, or oral corticosteroids; rituximab is less effective than in NMOSD, and MS drugs such as interferons, glatiramer acetate, and fingolimod appear ineffective.

Drugs for spasticity, bladder dysfunction, orthostatic hypotension, and sleep behavior

Neurological disease often leaves symptoms that need their own treatment, whatever the cause. Baclofen and botulinum toxin relax overactive muscles, antimuscarinics, mirabegron, and α-blockers treat bladder dysfunction, midodrine and fludrocortisone raise standing blood pressure, and melatonin treats REM sleep behavior disorder. Here, we review these drugs by target, mechanism, use, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • Abrupt withdrawal of baclofen causes seizures, hallucinations, and hyperthermia.
  • Botulinum toxin treats focal dystonia and sialorrhea and is avoided in myasthenia gravis and Lambert-Eaton syndrome.
  • Oxybutynin crosses the BBB and causes confusion in older adults, and it opposes cholinesterase inhibitors.
  • Midodrine and fludrocortisone both cause supine hypertension, and melatonin is tried before clonazepam for RBD.

Muscles

Baclofen activates GABA-B receptors in the spinal cord, reducing excitatory transmitter release onto motor neurons and so spasticity. It treats spasticity in MS and dystonia in atypical parkinsonism. It is renally cleared, so the dose is lowered in renal impairment, and it is given by mouth or intrathecally. It causes sedation, weakness, and dizziness, adds to other CNS depressants, and its abrupt withdrawal causes seizures, hallucinations, and hyperthermia.

Botulinum toxin cleaves SNARE proteins in cholinergic nerve terminals, blocking ACh release, which causes local weakness or less secretion for about 3 months, until terminals sprout. Injected locally, it treats focal dystonia, including in HD, and sialorrhea in atypical parkinsonism. It is avoided with infection at the site and in myasthenia gravis and Lambert-Eaton syndrome. It can weaken nearby muscles, causing dysphagia or ptosis, and aminoglycosides strengthen the block.

Bladder

Antimuscarinics, such as oxybutynin, block ACh at M3 receptors on the detrusor, reducing uninhibited contractions. They treat urge incontinence in MS, and selective antimuscarinics treat urinary dysfunction in atypical parkinsonism. Oxybutynin crosses the BBB, whereas selective drugs cause less confusion. Urinary retention, angle-closure glaucoma, and myasthenia gravis are contraindications, and they cause dry mouth, constipation, retention, and confusion in older adults; they add to the anticholinergic burden and oppose cholinesterase inhibitors.

Mirabegron activates β3 receptors on the detrusor, relaxing it and enlarging bladder capacity, and treats urinary dysfunction in atypical parkinsonism. It is a moderate CYP2D6 inhibitor, raising levels of substrates such as metoprolol. Severe uncontrolled hypertension is a contraindication, and it causes hypertension and urinary retention.

α-blockers, such as tamsulosin, block α1 receptors in the bladder neck and prostate, relaxing smooth muscle at the outlet so the bladder empties more easily, and treat urinary dysfunction in atypical parkinsonism. Tamsulosin is selective for α1A. Orthostatic hypotension is a contraindication, and they cause orthostatic hypotension, dizziness, and IFIS during cataract surgery, with additive hypotension alongside PDE-5 inhibitors and other antihypertensives.

Blood pressure and sleep

Midodrine is a prodrug of an α1 agonist that constricts arterioles and veins, raising standing BP, and treats orthostatic hypotension in atypical parkinsonism. It is short acting and given during the day only. Severe heart disease, urinary retention, and supine hypertension are contraindications, and it causes supine hypertension, piloerection, scalp itching, and urinary retention, with additive bradycardia alongside beta-blockers.

Fludrocortisone activates mineralocorticoid receptors in the kidney, increasing sodium and water retention, which expands plasma volume and raises BP, and it treats orthostatic hypotension in atypical parkinsonism. It is long acting and taken once daily. Heart failure is a contraindication, and it causes hypokalemia, which adds to digoxin toxicity and diuretic losses, edema, supine hypertension, and heart failure.

Melatonin activates MT1 and MT2 receptors in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, reducing RBD episodes, and is first line for RBD before clonazepam. It has a short half-life and is taken at bedtime. It causes morning drowsiness and headache, and fluvoxamine raises its levels.

Relationships to other conditions

Multiple sclerosis. Chronic symptoms are treated alongside disease-modifying therapy: baclofen for spasticity, oxybutynin for incontinence and catheterization for retention, and gabapentin for neuropathic pain.

Atypical parkinsonism and DLB. No treatment slows MSA, PSP, or CBD, so autonomic and other symptoms are treated directly: midodrine or fludrocortisone for orthostatic hypotension, α-blockers, mirabegron, or selective antimuscarinics for urinary dysfunction, botulinum toxin for dystonia and sialorrhea, and melatonin and then low-dose clonazepam for RBD, as in DLB.

Drugs for dementia, myasthenia, and mood in neurological disease

Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine give modest symptomatic benefit in Alzheimer disease and the Lewy body dementias, while anti-amyloid antibodies slow decline in early Alzheimer disease. Pyridostigmine treats myasthenia gravis, and antidepressants treat the depression, anxiety, and behavioral change that accompany neurodegenerative disease. Here, we review these drugs by target, mechanism, use, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • Cholinesterase inhibitors help Alzheimer disease, DLB, and dementia in Parkinson disease, but not FTD, where donepezil can worsen behavior.
  • Cholinesterase inhibitors cause bradycardia and syncope, which beta-blockers worsen.
  • Anti-amyloid antibodies need confirmed amyloid and MRI monitoring because of ARIA, which is more common in APOE4 carriers.
  • SSRIs cause serotonin syndrome with MAOIs, including high-dose rasagiline.

Dementia

Cholinesterase inhibitors, donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine, slow ACh breakdown by AChE, offsetting the loss of cholinergic neurons, with modest benefit in cognition and function and fewer hallucinations in DLB. They treat mild to moderate Alzheimer disease, DLB, and dementia in PD, but not FTD, where donepezil can worsen behavior. Donepezil has a long half-life and is taken once daily, and rivastigmine comes as a patch. Sick sinus syndrome or heart block without a pacemaker calls for caution. They cause nausea, diarrhea, bradycardia, syncope, and vivid dreams and can worsen motor function in PSP; anticholinergics oppose them, and beta-blockers add to the bradycardia.

Memantine blocks NMDA channels noncompetitively, limiting excitotoxicity, with modest benefit in moderate to severe Alzheimer disease, and it may add benefit in DLB. It is renally cleared, so the dose is lowered in renal impairment. It causes dizziness, headache, confusion, and constipation, and its effects add to those of other NMDA antagonists such as ketamine and amantadine.

Anti-amyloid antibodies, lecanemab and donanemab, bind amyloid β and clear plaques, slowing decline in early Alzheimer disease with confirmed amyloid. They are IV infusions with MRI monitoring. Anticoagulation, many microbleeds, and cerebral amyloid angiopathy are contraindications, and anticoagulants and thrombolytics raise the risk of hemorrhage. They cause ARIA, edema or microhemorrhage, more often in APOE4 carriers, and infusion reactions.

Pyridostigmine blocks AChE at the neuromuscular junction, prolonging ACh at nicotinic receptors and strengthening contractions, and it is first line for myasthenia gravis before immunosuppression. It is quaternary, so it does not cross the BBB. Mechanical bowel or urinary obstruction is a contraindication. It causes cramps, diarrhea, salivation, and bradycardia, excess causes cholinergic crisis, and anticholinergics oppose it.

Mood

SSRIs, such as sertraline and citalopram, block the serotonin transporter, so more serotonin stays in the synapse. They treat behavior in FTD, though not cognition, and depression and anxiety in PD, DLB, and HD. They take weeks to work fully, and citalopram prolongs the QT interval at higher doses. MAOI use within 2 weeks is a contraindication. They cause GI upset, sexual dysfunction, hyponatremia, bleeding, and serotonin syndrome, which follows their use with MAOIs, including high-dose rasagiline, and tramadol, and antiplatelets and anticoagulants add to the bleeding.

Mirtazapine blocks presynaptic α2 receptors, increasing norepinephrine and serotonin release, and also blocks 5-HT2 and 5-HT3 receptors, giving an antidepressant effect with sedation and weight gain. It treats depression in DLB and is sedating at low doses. MAOI use is a contraindication, with which it causes serotonin syndrome, and it causes sedation, weight gain, and, rarely, agranulocytosis.

Relationships to other conditions

Alzheimer disease and DLB. Both respond to cholinesterase inhibitors, often better in DLB, where they also reduce hallucinations, and memantine is added in both. DLB adds a severe sensitivity to antipsychotics, whereas anti-amyloid antibodies are used only in early Alzheimer disease with confirmed amyloid.

Frontotemporal dementia. Unlike Alzheimer disease, FTD does not respond to cholinesterase inhibitors or memantine, so SSRIs treat behavior, and antipsychotics give mixed results, with patients prone to extrapyramidal effects.

Drugs for Parkinson disease, chorea, and tremor

Parkinson disease is treated by raising dopamine signaling, with levodopa, dopamine agonists, and inhibitors of dopamine and levodopa breakdown, while amantadine and anticholinergics add benefit. Chorea in Huntington disease is treated by lowering dopamine signaling, with VMAT2 inhibitors or antipsychotics, and essential tremor responds to propranolol. Here, we review these drugs by target, mechanism, use, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • Levodopa with carbidopa is the most effective drug for PD; carbidopa blocks peripheral DDC, and wearing off develops as neurons are lost.
  • Dopamine agonists such as pramipexole are the main trigger of impulse control disorders.
  • VMAT2 inhibitors are first line for chorea but cause depression and suicidality.
  • Antipsychotics cause parkinsonism, most with haloperidol and fluphenazine and least with quetiapine and clozapine, and are avoided in DLB.

Raising dopamine

Levodopa with carbidopa raises striatal dopamine: levodopa crosses the BBB and becomes dopamine, while carbidopa blocks peripheral DDC, so less is converted outside the brain. Bradykinesia and rigidity lessen. It is the most effective drug for PD, is tried in atypical parkinsonism, is used at low doses in DLB, and treats rigidity in HD. Its short half-life means that, as neurons are lost, on time shortens, which is wearing off, and dietary protein competes with it for absorption and transport across the BBB. Nonselective MAOIs within 2 weeks, with which it causes hypertensive crisis, and angle-closure glaucoma are contraindications. It causes nausea, orthostatic hypotension, dyskinesia, hallucinations, and motor fluctuations, and D2 antagonists oppose it.

Dopamine agonists, such as pramipexole, stimulate striatal D2 and D3 receptors directly, reducing bradykinesia. They treat PD, especially early or in younger patients, and rigidity in HD. They are longer acting than levodopa and renally cleared. Psychosis and impulse control disorder are contraindications, and they cause impulse control disorders, including gambling, hypersexuality, shopping, and binge eating, as well as sleep attacks, hallucinations, and edema; antipsychotics oppose them.

MAO-B inhibitors, such as rasagiline, irreversibly inhibit MAO-B, the main form in the brain, slowing dopamine breakdown and prolonging its effect. They treat PD early or as an add-on, and they are selective at usual doses, so dietary tyramine is safe. Other MAOIs, meperidine, and tramadol are contraindicated, and with SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, meperidine, and tramadol they cause serotonin syndrome. They cause insomnia, orthostatic hypotension, and worse dyskinesia with levodopa.

COMT inhibitors, such as entacapone, block peripheral COMT, so each dose of levodopa lasts longer, and they treat wearing off. They only work with levodopa, whose exposure they raise. Nonselective MAOIs are contraindicated, and they cause dyskinesia, diarrhea, and orange urine.

Amantadine increases dopamine release and blocks its reuptake, with weak NMDA antagonism, easing bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, and dyskinesia, often only briefly. It treats PD and rigidity in HD. It is renally cleared and accumulates in renal impairment, so severe renal impairment is a contraindication. It causes livedo reticularis, ankle edema, confusion, and hallucinations, with additive anticholinergic and NMDA effects.

Anticholinergics, trihexyphenidyl and benztropine, block striatal muscarinic receptors, restoring the balance between dopamine and ACh and reducing tremor and dystonia. Trihexyphenidyl treats tremor in younger patients with PD, and benztropine treats drug-induced parkinsonism and acute dystonia. They cross the BBB, so dementia and older age, as well as angle-closure glaucoma and urinary retention, are contraindications. They cause dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, blurred vision, and confusion, add to the anticholinergic burden, and oppose cholinesterase inhibitors.

Lowering dopamine

VMAT2 inhibitors, tetrabenazine, deutetrabenazine, and valbenazine, block loading of dopamine into presynaptic vesicles, so less is released and chorea lessens, and they are first line for chorea in HD. They are metabolized by CYP2D6, so inhibitors such as fluoxetine raise their levels, and deutetrabenazine lasts longer, needing fewer doses. Untreated depression or suicidality, MAOIs, and liver impairment are contraindications. They cause depression and suicidality, fatigue, akathisia, parkinsonism, and QT prolongation, which adds to that of other drugs.

Antipsychotics, including haloperidol, risperidone, olanzapine, aripiprazole, quetiapine, and clozapine, block D2 receptors in mesolimbic and striatal pathways, and the newer drugs also block 5-HT2A, reducing psychosis and chorea but causing parkinsonism when striatal block is strong. They treat chorea in HD off label, and quetiapine or clozapine treats psychosis in PD. Extrapyramidal effects are most likely with haloperidol and fluphenazine, then risperidone and olanzapine, and least with quetiapine and clozapine. They are avoided in DLB, where serious reactions occur in up to 50%, and in dementia-related psychosis in older adults. They cause drug-induced parkinsonism, acute dystonia, akathisia, tardive dyskinesia, NMS, QT prolongation, and metabolic syndrome, and clozapine causes agranulocytosis; they oppose levodopa and dopamine agonists, and QT prolongation is additive.

Pimavanserin is an inverse agonist at 5-HT2A receptors that does not block dopamine, so it reduces psychosis without worsening parkinsonism, and it treats psychosis in PD. It has a long half-life and is metabolized by CYP3A4, so strong CYP3A4 inhibitors raise its levels. QT prolongation is a contraindication, and it causes peripheral edema, confusion, nausea, and QT prolongation.

Tremor

Propranolol blocks β receptors, damping peripheral and central tremor oscillation and reducing tremor amplitude, and it treats essential tremor, with primidone as the alternative. It is lipophilic, crosses the BBB, and has high first-pass metabolism. Asthma, bradycardia, heart block, and decompensated heart failure are contraindications, and it causes bradycardia, fatigue, bronchospasm, depression, and masked hypoglycemia, with additive AV block alongside verapamil and diltiazem.

Parkinson disease and Huntington disease. Rigidity in the Westphal variant of HD is treated like PD, with levodopa, dopamine agonists, or amantadine.

Analgesics and anesthetics acting on the pain pathway

Analgesics act at every level of the pain pathway, from prostaglandin synthesis at the site of injury to transmission in the dorsal horn and descending inhibition from the brainstem, while anesthetics block conduction or depress the brain. Opioids, paracetamol, NSAIDs, SNRIs and TCAs, gabapentinoids, ketamine, local anesthetics, and propofol each have characteristic hazards, especially respiratory depression. Here, we review their targets, uses, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • Opioids shift the CO2 response curve right, whereas benzodiazepines and propofol flatten it, and together they add to respiratory depression.
  • Paracetamol replaces aspirin and NSAIDs for pain relief in SAH.
  • SNRIs, TCAs, and gabapentinoids treat neuropathic pain, and TCAs are lethal in overdose.
  • Local anesthetic toxicity starts with perioral numbness and seizures and progresses to arrhythmia and cardiac arrest.

Analgesics

Opioids, such as morphine and fentanyl, act on μ-opioid receptors in the PAG, RVM, dorsal horn, and peripheral afferents, opening potassium channels and closing N-type calcium channels, and in the PAG they release descending inhibition, giving analgesia for severe pain. Morphine metabolites accumulate in renal failure, and fentanyl is lipophilic and potent, with patches lasting 3 days. Respiratory depression and paralytic ileus are contraindications. They cause respiratory depression, shifting the CO2 response curve right, as well as constipation, nausea, miosis, sedation, tolerance, and dependence; benzodiazepines and alcohol add to the respiratory depression, and naloxone reverses them.

Paracetamol inhibits central COX and strengthens descending serotonergic inhibition, also acting on endocannabinoid pathways, giving analgesia and antipyresis with little anti-inflammatory effect. It relieves pain in SAH, in place of aspirin and NSAIDs, and treats fever after stroke. It is metabolized in the liver, and a small fraction becomes a toxic metabolite detoxified by glutathione, so overdose causes hepatotoxicity, chronic alcohol raises that risk, and severe liver disease is a contraindication.

NSAIDs, such as ibuprofen, aspirin, and celecoxib, decrease prostaglandin synthesis through COX-1 and COX-2, and aspirin binds irreversibly, giving analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory effects; celecoxib selects COX-2. They treat pain and inflammation but are avoided in SAH. Because it spares COX-1, celecoxib causes fewer ulcers but raises cardiovascular risk. Peptic ulcer or bleeding, renal impairment, and late pregnancy are contraindications, and celecoxib is avoided in cardiovascular disease and sulfa allergy. They cause GI ulcers and bleeding, kidney injury, and fluid retention, anticoagulants and SSRIs add to the bleeding, and they raise lithium levels.

SNRIs and TCAs, such as duloxetine and amitriptyline, block serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake, raising both in the spinal cord and strengthening descending inhibition. They treat neuropathic pain and depression in DLB and HD, and duloxetine treats fibromyalgia. Amitriptyline also blocks muscarinic, H1, and α1 receptors and sodium channels. MAOI use is a contraindication, with which they cause serotonin syndrome, as with other serotonergic drugs, and TCAs are avoided after recent MI and in heart block. Duloxetine causes nausea and hypertension, and TCAs cause anticholinergic effects, sedation, orthostatic hypotension, and QRS widening and are lethal in overdose.

Gabapentinoids, gabapentin and pregabalin, bind the α2δ-1 subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing calcium entry and excitatory transmitter release and relieving neuropathic pain, including in MS. They are renally cleared unchanged, so the dose is lowered in renal impairment. They cause sedation, dizziness, edema, and misuse, and they add to the respiratory depression of opioids.

Anesthetics

Ketamine blocks NMDA glutamate receptors noncompetitively, giving analgesia and dissociative anesthesia with breathing preserved. It acts fast and raises sympathetic tone, so uncontrolled hypertension is a contraindication, as is psychosis. It causes emergence hallucinations, hypertension, tachycardia, and increased secretions, and its effects add to those of other NMDA antagonists.

Local anesthetics, such as lidocaine, block voltage-gated sodium channels from inside the channel, so conduction fails and pain signals stop, giving local and regional anesthesia. Small, myelinated pain fibers block first, adrenaline prolongs the block by vasoconstriction, and amides are metabolized in the liver, so beta-blockers and cimetidine raise lidocaine levels. Amide allergy is rare. Systemic toxicity begins with perioral numbness and seizures and progresses to arrhythmia and cardiac arrest.

Propofol potentiates GABA-A receptors, giving rapid sedation and anesthesia for surgery and ICU sedation, with fast onset and offset through redistribution. Egg or soy allergy calls for caution. It causes respiratory depression, flattening the CO2 response curve, hypotension, and propofol infusion syndrome, and opioids and benzodiazepines add to the respiratory depression.

Drugs for glaucoma, macular degeneration, and conjunctivitis, and those that cause angle closure

Glaucoma drugs lower intraocular pressure either by increasing outflow of aqueous humor or by decreasing its production, and their adverse effects follow from local and systemic absorption. Anti-VEGF agents treat wet macular degeneration, topical antibiotics treat bacterial conjunctivitis, and several drugs that dilate the pupil can trigger acute angle closure. Here, we review these drugs by target, mechanism, use, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • Prostaglandin analogs are first line for open-angle glaucoma and darken the iris and lengthen eyelashes.
  • Timolol drops are absorbed systemically and are avoided in asthma, COPD, heart block, and bradycardia.
  • Acute angle closure is treated with acetazolamide, timolol, and apraclonidine, with mannitol if needed, and pilocarpine once IOP is below 40 mmHg.
  • Mydriatics, sympathomimetics, anticholinergics, H1 antagonists, and topiramate can trigger angle closure.

Increasing outflow

Prostaglandin analogs, such as latanoprost, act on FP receptors in the ciliary muscle to increase uveoscleral outflow, lowering IOP. They are first line for open-angle glaucoma, as once-daily drops. Active uveitis calls for caution, and they darken the iris and periorbital skin, lengthen eyelashes, and redden the conjunctiva.

Cholinergic agents, such as pilocarpine, act on M3 receptors in the ciliary muscle and iris sphincter, contracting the ciliary muscle and constricting the pupil, which opens the trabecular meshwork and increases outflow. Pilocarpine treats acute angle closure once IOP is below 40 mmHg, because an ischemic iris sphincter does not respond, and open-angle glaucoma, though it is poorly tolerated, needing drops several times a day. Uveitis is a contraindication, and it causes brow ache, dim vision from miosis, and myopia.

Decreasing production

β-blockers, such as timolol, block β receptors on the ciliary epithelium, reducing aqueous production, and treat open-angle glaucoma and acute angle closure. The drops are absorbed systemically through the nasolacrimal duct, causing bradycardia and bronchospasm, so asthma, COPD, heart block, and bradycardia are contraindications, and oral beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers add to the bradycardia.

α2-agonists, brimonidine and apraclonidine, act on α2 receptors on the ciliary epithelium, decreasing aqueous production while increasing uveoscleral outflow. They treat open-angle glaucoma, and apraclonidine treats acute angle closure. Brimonidine crosses the BBB and causes apnea and CNS depression in children, so infants and young children should not receive it, and MAOI use is a contraindication. They also cause allergic conjunctivitis and dry mouth.

Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, dorzolamide and acetazolamide, inhibit carbonic anhydrase in the ciliary epithelium, reducing bicarbonate and so aqueous production. Acetazolamide 500 mg, by mouth or IV, treats acute angle closure, and dorzolamide drops treat open-angle glaucoma. Sulfa allergy calls for caution, and acetazolamide is avoided in hypokalemia and severe liver disease. Acetazolamide causes paresthesia, metabolic acidosis, hypokalemia, and kidney stones, and topiramate and high-dose salicylates add to the acidosis; dorzolamide causes a bitter taste and stinging.

Other eye drugs

Anti-VEGF agents, ranibizumab and aflibercept, bind VEGF and stop new leaky vessels, reducing fluid and bleeding under the retina in wet AMD. They are injected into the eye every 1–3 months. Eye or periocular infection is a contraindication, and they cause endophthalmitis, raised IOP, and retinal detachment.

Topical antibiotics, including chloramphenicol, polymyxin B with trimethoprim, and fluoroquinolones such as moxifloxacin, kill or stop bacteria on the ocular surface by inhibiting protein synthesis or DNA gyrase, shortening bacterial conjunctivitis. They are used 4 times daily for 5–7 days and act locally, causing stinging and local allergy.

Drugs that can trigger angle closure

Mydriatics and cycloplegics, atropine and cyclopentolate, and sympathomimetics, adrenaline, phenylephrine, and pseudoephedrine, dilate the pupil and can trigger angle closure, as can anticholinergics and H1 antagonists. Topiramate triggers it differently, by swelling the ciliary body so that the lens and iris are pushed forward.

Pilocarpine waits until IOP is below 40 mmHg, because an ischemic iris sphincter does not respond.

Relationships to other conditions

Open-angle glaucoma and acute angle closure. Open-angle glaucoma starts with a prostaglandin analog, then laser trabeculoplasty and surgery, whereas acute angle closure is an emergency treated with acetazolamide, timolol, and apraclonidine, mannitol if needed, and pilocarpine, followed by laser peripheral iridotomy, the treatment of choice.

Vision loss and red eye. Wet AMD is treated with intravitreal anti-VEGF agents, without which most eyes become legally blind, and bacterial conjunctivitis, often self-limited, is shortened by topical antibiotics.

Drugs for vertigo and Ménière disease

Vestibular suppressants relieve acute vertigo but delay central compensation, so they are used only briefly, while Ménière disease is treated by reducing endolymphatic hydrops and, when refractory, by destroying vestibular hair cells. Here, we review meclizine and other suppressants, betahistine, thiazides, and intratympanic gentamicin by target, mechanism, use, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • Vestibular suppressants are given for about 3 days in vestibular neuritis and labyrinthitis, because longer use delays central compensation.
  • Ménière disease is treated with less salt, caffeine, and alcohol, thiazides or betahistine, and intratympanic gentamicin if refractory.
  • Aminoglycosides, loop diuretics, and cisplatin are ototoxic, and high-dose aspirin causes reversible tinnitus.

Vertigo and Ménière disease

Vestibular suppressants, such as meclizine, as well as benzodiazepines and antiemetics, block histamine and ACh at H1 and muscarinic receptors in the vestibular nuclei, reducing vestibular signaling and so vertigo and nausea. They are given for about 3 days in vestibular neuritis and labyrinthitis and have little role in BPPV, because beyond a few days they delay central compensation. Angle-closure glaucoma and urinary retention are contraindications. They cause sedation, which alcohol and other CNS depressants worsen, dry mouth, and confusion in older adults.

Betahistine is a weak agonist at H1 receptors and an antagonist at H3 receptors; it improves inner ear microcirculation and reduces vestibular nucleus firing, so attacks of vertigo are fewer, and it treats Ménière disease. Its short half-life means several doses a day, and antihistamines oppose it. Pheochromocytoma is a contraindication, and it causes headache and GI upset.

Thiazides, such as hydrochlorothiazide, block NCC in the distal convoluted tubule, lowering body fluid and endolymphatic hydrops, and treat Ménière disease alongside less salt, caffeine, and alcohol. They are taken once daily. Sulfa allergy calls for caution, and gout is a contraindication. They cause hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hypercalcemia, hyperuricemia, and hyperglycemia, raise lithium levels, and their hypokalemia adds to digoxin toxicity.

Intratympanic gentamicin is an aminoglycoside injected through the eardrum that destroys vestibular hair cells, a chemical labyrinthectomy, for Ménière disease not controlled otherwise. Bilateral disease is a contraindication, and it causes hearing loss and imbalance.

Aminoglycosides, loop diuretics, and cisplatin are ototoxic, and high-dose aspirin causes reversible tinnitus.

Vertigo. Treatment targets the cause: the Epley maneuver for BPPV, brief suppressants and then vestibular rehabilitation for vestibular neuritis and labyrinthitis, and the measures above for Ménière disease; suppressants only relieve acute symptoms.

Hearing loss. Drugs both treat and cause it: oral prednisone treats sudden SNHL, an emergency, whereas aminoglycosides, loop diuretics, and cisplatin cause sensorineural loss that may be permanent, and intratympanic gentamicin deliberately destroys vestibular function at the risk of hearing loss.
